Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth: Living Review

2026-10-18 · Adrien Joly

Latest research

New papers on AI and labour markets and AI and growth, newest first, each placed against the review below. Every figure is checked against the paper itself. This section is updated automatically and is not reviewed by Adrien before it appears.

Domnisoru and Heikkinen (2026), "How is AI Affecting College Major and Course Choices? Evidence from Finland"

RFBerlin Discussion Paper 293/26, November 2026. Paper. Descriptive event studies on administrative data, outside the review's causal evidence standard; reported as context.

What it finds. Domnisoru and Heikkinen (2026) find that first-choice applicants to Finnish university degrees in computer science and other ICT fields fell 38.0% between the 2022 and 2026 rounds, while applicants to all other fields rose 16.5%. They use applications by Finnish citizens for 2018 to 2026 and give each degree the automation exposure of the occupations its graduates held in 2021 and 2022, measured from observed Claude use. First-choice applicants to the most exposed quarter of degrees fell 16.6% over the same period, against a 20.3% rise for the other three quarters. Within broad fields, a degree one standard deviation more exposed had 14.0% fewer first-choice applicants in 2026, relative to 2022, and differences before 2022 are not distinguishable from zero. Measures based on rated task automatability give about 5 points.

Where it fits.

  • Entry (section “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”). Orr et al. (2026) measure what happens to US graduates of exposed majors; this paper measures the step before, the choice of degree. Among Finnish master's graduates under 30, one standard deviation more exposure goes with an employment rate 1 point lower and unemployment 0.7 points higher by 2026, relative to 2022. Within firms the gaps between exposed and less exposed occupations largely disappear up to March 2026, in line with the designs that find no effect on total employment.

  • Expectations against outcomes. Unemployment among young ICT graduates rose 2.9 points, from 2.7% to 5.6%, against the 38% fall in applicants, and controlling for field unemployment barely moves the estimates. The authors read this as a response to expected rather than current conditions, the gap Sacher et al. (2026) measure in surveys.

  • Mobility, κₒ (Main patterns). Adjustment happens at entry: switching between degrees does not rise in the most exposed quarter of degrees after 2022, and switching requires a new application. Among enrolled students, take-up of AI and machine learning electives rose from 0.3% to 0.7%.

Caveat. The authors state that the estimates are not causal. Applications are not enrolment: ICT programmes went from about two applicants per place in 2019 to about one in 2026, but enrolment has not generally fallen. Finland's centralised applications and costly switching may make the response larger or more abrupt than elsewhere.

Strauss, Romei and Fleming (2026), "What is behind the surprising resilience of the UK economy?"

Financial Times, 17 October 2026, with the ONS GDP monthly estimate for August 2026 (15 October 2026). News analysis of official data, outside the review's causal evidence standard; reported as context for AI and growth.

What it finds. UK GDP grew 1.8% in the three months to August 2026 compared with a year earlier, the fastest since 2022, and AI-related business services lead it. In the ONS release, computer programming, consultancy and related activities added 0.32 percentage points, about a sixth of that growth and the largest contribution of any industry. The FT reports professional services up 6.6% on a year earlier and the biggest contributor. Consumer-facing services stay weak: hospitality shrank 1% over the year and consumer services remain 6% below their pre-pandemic level, while construction fell 1.6%. Payroll employment fell over the year in both the tech sector and professional services.

Where it fits.

  • Output without hires, κₒ and the margin of adjustment (Main patterns). The industries growing fastest are cutting payroll employment. This matches the review's finding that AI shows up as fewer hires in exposed work rather than layoffs, including in the UK, where more exposed firms employ fewer juniors (Klein Teeselink 2025; section Exposure designs: hiring and entry).

  • Demand for expert work, ηₒ (Main patterns). Growth comes from firms selling AI adoption and advice to other firms, much of it exported. It is the aggregate side of Garicano (2026), where demand for lawyers, accountants and software developers grows as clients adopt AI (section Firm adoption studies), although there employment grew too.

  • Growth. The gain so far sits in industries that produce and sell AI services, not in the economy-wide productivity of adopters. Economists quoted expect that second step, with employment and real wages recovering only in late 2027.

Caveat. Industry contributions are growth accounting, not causal estimates. The ONS release does not attribute the growth to AI; the link to AI comes from the FT and the economists it quotes, and computer programming also covers non-AI work.

Anthropic's third-quarter revenue (Reuters and Financial Times, 14 October 2026)

Reuters (report), citing the Financial Times, with earlier quarters from The Next Web, 15 October 2026. News report of unaudited figures given to investors; reported as context on demand for AI services.

What it finds. Anthropic told investors its revenue reached $16.8bn in the third quarter of 2026, after about $11.6bn in the second and $4.7bn in the first, and that it turned a profit and was cash-flow positive in the quarter. Revenue thus rose about 150% from the first to the second quarter and about 45% from the second to the third. Bloomberg reports a stock market listing could follow in November.

Where it fits.

  • Diffusion, bₒΔa (Main patterns). Firms' spending on AI services, the cost share bₒ, is rising fast at one of the largest providers, which tripled its quarterly revenue in six months. Quarterly growth is slowing, from about 150% to 45%.

  • Growth. Revenue of this size is what the AI investment boom is betting on: it is the demand side of the capital spending financed in the next entry.

Caveat. One firm's unaudited figures, disclosed to investors ahead of a listing. This review is maintained with Claude, Anthropic's model.

CoreWeave's bonds trade at distressed yields (CNBC, 14 October 2026)

CNBC reporting, via investingLive, 15 October 2026, with LSEG and S&P Global data. News report; context on how AI capital spending is financed.

What it finds. On 14 October, shares of CoreWeave, a cloud provider that rents out AI chips, fell about 16%, and its bonds due 2032 yielded about 14.8%, against a 9.625% coupon. S&P rates the firm B+, below investment grade, and says its capital spending is "close to hyperscaler scale without the balance sheet of a hyperscaler". Its chief executive told staff that the backlog of customer contracts covers all its debt. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle had issued more than $228bn of bonds in 2026 by mid-October, more than double their total for 2025 (LSEG).

Where it fits.

  • Growth. AI investment is increasingly financed by debt, and the stress shows first at the firms without large cash flows. In the AI correction scenarios of the IMF (2026) and the Bank of England (2026), the financial channel costs more output than the direct loss of investment, which falling imports partly offset.

  • Price of AI services, Δa (Main patterns). The fall in the price of AI services that drives the review's equation depends on this capacity being built. Dearer financing would slow it.

Caveat. Market moves over one day, reported second hand.

Sacher, Cotter, Jones, Korinek and McCrory (2026), "The AI Expectations Gap"

Anthropic Institute, 16 October 2026. Report. Descriptive survey, outside the review's causal evidence standard; reported as context.

What it finds. US workers expect AI to be able to do 28% of their current work hours by 2030, against 75% for Anthropic staff. The authors survey 10,980 US adults through Morning Consult (11 to 23 August 2026, weighted to be nationally representative) and poll 1,334 Anthropic staff (unweighted). Told that unemployment is about 4.2%, workers who expect AI to do less than half of their work forecast 7 to 8% unemployment in 2030, and those who expect it to do 80% or more forecast 12.2%; ten more points of own-work exposure go with 0.4 points more forecast unemployment, a gradient staff share. Mapped into the Economic Scenarios model, the median adult's answers imply 5.4% GDP growth in 2030 and the median staff member's 17.1%.

Where it fits.

  • Expectations against outcomes (employment). Workers expect far more unemployment than the evidence shows so far. Hartley et al. (2026) find the same gap: the average US worker puts a 20% chance on losing their job to GenAI within two years, yet postings and layoffs in exposed occupations show no significant response. In the review, designs that measure total employment find no effect, and the fall is in young hires (section “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”).

  • Time saved, πₒ (Main patterns). The survey asks directly for gₒ, the share of labour time AI saves. Among US adults who use AI at work, the typical answer is that a suited task takes half the time, a doubling of output per hour; 85% of staff say half the time or less. Both exceed the gains measured in field experiments, such as 14% more resolutions per hour in customer support (section “Field experiments”), although those measure whole jobs, not tasks chosen as suited to AI.

  • Diffusion, bₒΔa (Main patterns). US adults expect 42% of the tasks AI can do to be done with AI by 2030, staff 65%, against 30 to 40% of US workers using GenAI at work today (Hartley et al. 2026).

  • Growth. The model is that of Korinek et al. (2026) in the AI and growth review, by the same team. The median adult sits on its Substantial path (5.4% growth in 2030); the median staff member sits above its Extreme path (15.4%).

Caveat. Staff answers are personal views, not company forecasts, and staff work at a firm that sells AI. The only representative sample is of US adults, drawn from an opt-in online panel.

Hyman, Lahey, Ni and Pilossoph (2025, revised August 2026), "How Retrainable are AI-Exposed Workers?"

NBER Working Paper 34174. Paper. Matched comparison on administrative data, resting on selection on observed characteristics; the split of returns by occupational move is descriptive.

What it finds. Hyman et al. (2025) find that the quarterly earnings return to US public job training for workers leaving highly AI-exposed occupations rose from about $1,000 before 2020 to about $3,000 by 2022 to 2024, in 2010 dollars. The return compares trainees with matched workers who sought help but received only job search assistance. The sample is 99,575 matched training spells under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act from 2012 to 2024, with earnings for three quarters before and after. High exposure means above the 55th percentile of the Eloundou et al. (2024) score. Over the whole period the return is about $1,200 a quarter for these workers and about $2,400 for workers from less exposed occupations. In 2022 to 2024, training made highly exposed workers over 20 percentage points more likely to move to a less exposed occupation than their matches.

Where it fits.

  • Mobility, κₒ (Main patterns). The review notes that how displaced workers would move is not yet observed. This paper observes one route, public training. Manning and Aguirre (2026) rate capacity to adapt from aggregate data; Hyman et al. use estimated returns. About 45% of occupations are “AI retrainable”, meaning their workers on average move to both higher earnings and higher exposure than their matches; these occupations cover 39% of the US labour force.

  • Direction of the move. Good outcomes run both ways. Workers from less exposed occupations gain by moving up in exposure, and training made them 23 points more likely to do so in 2022 to 2024, against 17 points in 2012 to 2019. Highly exposed workers gain more by moving down, often into roles such as nursing assistant.

  • Size of the sorting channel. Summed over both groups, moves in exposure account for about 3% of returns before the AI boom and 11% during it, so most of the return does not come from this channel. The rise for highly exposed workers is different: the authors attribute it mainly to more moves into less exposed occupations, and they read this decomposition as descriptive.

Caveat. Trainees may differ from their matches in ways the data do not show, such as motivation. Occupation codes exist for only 22% of spells, and the main sample keeps only reemployed workers. Earnings are followed for three quarters. Less than 1% of participants are in the youth programme, so the results describe displaced adults, not young entrants. The 2022 to 2024 period also had tight labour markets.

Althoff and Reichardt (2026), "Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage"

NBER Working Paper 35353, 22 June 2026; figures from the authors' version of 13 July 2026. Paper. Quantified model of future AI, not evidence on observed effects; reported as context.

What it finds. Althoff and Reichardt (2026) find that AI lowers US wage inequality and raises average wages in their model, because AI can simplify tasks as well as augment and automate them. Simplifying means lowering the skill a task requires, so lower-skill workers can compete for jobs that were closed to them. The model is estimated on the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979, a worker panel, with language models rating what AI can do in each task. In the moderate scenario, the new steady state compared with no AI has a 90-10 wage ratio 24% lower (13% slow, 33% rapid) and average wages 27.6% higher. Without simplification, inequality would rise slightly. With adoption costs that are highest for lower-skill workers, the ratio falls 15% and average wages rise only 0.3%.

Where it fits.

  • Eligibility, Δln psₒ (Main patterns). Simplification is close to the eligibility shift of Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026), who measure it only by language model ratings. Althoff and Reichardt also rely on such ratings, but report that occupations with larger predicted simplification show falling skill requirements in US job postings. The sign differs: productivity gains and eligibility together widen the 90-50 gap in Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026), while the 90-10 ratio falls here. The two use different models and percentiles.

  • Skills. Verbal skills lose the most value, while manual and math skills keep much of theirs. Majors high in manual and technical skill, such as mechanics and engineering, gain over 1.5 points relative to other majors, and those high in verbal and social skill, such as law and philosophy, lose. Average skill levels fall, because workers learn less on simpler tasks.

  • Where the fall in entry ends. The occupations that gain most employment are often those whose relative wages fall most, because simplification enlarges the pool of qualified workers.

Caveat. These are model predictions, and AI capabilities are inputs rated by language models. The average wage result ranges from 27.6% to 0.3% depending on adoption costs. Careers run from age 25 to 64, so the model does not speak to the entry of younger workers.

Key concepts and definitions

Eloundou et al. (2024) define a task as directly exposed to GenAI if a large language model could cut the time needed to complete it by at least half at equivalent quality, meaning that the recipient of the output would not notice or care that AI was used. GenAI occupational exposure is the share of a job’s bundle of tasks that are directly exposed to AI, and a firm’s exposure is typically the average occupational exposure of its workforce just before ChatGPT’s release in November 2022.

Anthropic’s observed occupational exposure index combines the task ratings of Eloundou et al. (2024) with usage data from Claude [note 1]: computer and mathematical occupations are 94% exposed in theory but only 33% in observed use, and 30% of workers have no observed exposure at all (Massenkoff and McCrory 2026).

Adoption records whether a firm actually uses GenAI; studies date it from a firm’s vacancies, survey answers or spending on AI vendors (section “Firm adoption studies”).

Once a firm adopts, AI can change its employment through three channels: it is labour saving when it replaces some of a worker’s tasks, labour expanding when it creates new tasks such as prompt engineering or AI auditing, and it has a scale effect when the firm, made more productive, expands and hires more workers of every kind.

Applied micro evidence

Field experiments

Field experiments give workers access to GenAI and compare them with workers without it on the same task. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) study 5,179 customer support agents at a Fortune 500 business software firm, which rolled out a chat assistant built on a GPT model to different agents at different dates. Comparing each agent before and after access, they find that resolutions per hour rose by 14% on average and by 34% for the fifth of agents who were least productive before the tool, and that agents with two months of tenure performed similarly to untreated agents with six months; the assistant spread the practices of the best agents, and customers’ requests to speak to a manager fell by almost 25% from a baseline of about 6% of chats. Other experiments, including one in which software developers given a coding assistant completed 26% more tasks (Cui et al. 2026), find the largest gains for workers with lower baseline scores, shorter tenure, junior roles or less education (Noy and Zhang 2023; Dell’Acqua et al. 2023; Gambacorta et al. 2024; Cruces et al. 2026), although consultants using AI performed worse on a problem beyond the capabilities of GPT-4 (Dell’Acqua et al. 2023). Yet college graduates are twice as likely as others to use GenAI at work, which may widen inequality (Bick et al. 2024).

Worker surveys linked to registers

So far, these productivity gains have not raised total employment or pay. Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) survey 25,000 workers in 11 highly exposed occupations in late 2023 and 2024, and link their answers to the Danish employer register. In a monthly difference in differences around ChatGPT’s release, they compare chatbot users with other workers, and workplaces whose employers encourage chatbots with other workplaces: users’ own earnings and recorded hours are unchanged, and encouraging workplaces show no change in employment, wage bills or the hiring of early career workers. About 8% of chatbot users report entirely new tasks, such as drafting, checking AI output or integrating AI into workflows, rising to roughly 17% in workplaces where employers actively encourage chatbots, although these figures are self reported.

Exposure designs: hiring and entry

Exposure designs are difference in differences: they compare more and less exposed college majors, occupations, firms or industries before and after ChatGPT’s release. Orr et al. (2026) link records of 6.7 million US college graduates to Census Bureau employer and employee records, assign each major the exposure of its young graduates’ usual occupations before 2023, and compare the most exposed tenth of majors with the least exposed six tenths, relative to 2022. Graduates of the most exposed majors, mostly computer science, became 5 percentage points less likely to be employed in the quarter after graduation and earned 13% less when employed.

Tucker (2026) groups US Census employment data by industry and state and compares 22 to 24 year olds in the fifth of these groups most exposed to AI with those in less exposed groups, and with older workers in the same groups: their employment fell by 12% over ten quarters, mainly through fewer hires. Lodefalk et al. (2026) compare, within the same Swedish employer and month, employment in the most exposed quarter of occupations with that in less exposed ones, age group by age group, and find employment of 22 to 25 year olds 5.5% lower by early 2025, which they read as fewer young hires, although, observing employment rather than hires, they cannot rule out that Sweden’s “last in, first out” layoff rule pushed job cuts onto young workers. In France, Bergeaud, Bertheau, et al. (2026) use the records of every hire and contract end in private establishments from January 2019 to March 2026 (the Dares mouvements de main d’œuvre, built from employers’ monthly social security declarations), with an exposure index built by having a language model rate the 16,740 skills listed in France Travail’s directory of occupations. Between November 2022 and March 2026, hires of 21 to 29 year olds on permanent contracts fell by 28.3% in the most exposed fifth of occupations, against 7.5% in the least exposed fifth, after similar trends before 2022. The gap shrinks with age, from 20.8 points at 21 to 29 to 3.8 points after 50, and remains 17 points when software developers, whose young hires fell by 42%, are excluded. Contract ends among young workers in exposed occupations also fell, by 23% against 6%: firms stop hiring rather than lay off, and young workers change employer less often. The fall also appears at the firm level: matched to the Banque de France’s spring 2026 business survey, the roughly 600 firms that report intensive AI use cut permanent hires of 21 to 29 year olds by 17 to 25% between 2022 and 2025 compared with other firms in the same sector, with small and rarely significant gaps for older workers and no significant change in total headcount, although, as AI use is reported only in 2026, these firms may have been selected on hiring cuts they had already planned. Part of the fall may also be a shift to apprenticeships: French apprentices more than doubled, from about 430,000 in 2017 to over a million at the end of 2023, and among 21 to 29 year olds in the most exposed fifth of occupations their share rose from 6% at the end of 2019 to 10% at the end of 2023. Comparing UK firms, Klein Teeselink (2025) finds that one standard deviation more exposure lowers junior employment by 0.4%. In the Swiss unemployment register, the number of unemployed job seekers rose by up to 27% more in highly exposed occupations after autumn 2022 (Kläui and Siegenthaler 2025).

In job postings across 39 countries, Klein Teeselink and Carey (2026) find that one standard deviation more exposure lowers postings by 6.2%, more where employment protection is stricter; comparing occupations within the same economy, industry and month across 110 economies, Ando et al. (2026) find postings in more exposed occupations down nearly 10%.

Designs that measure total employment rather than hiring find no effect. Kauhanen and Rouvinen (2025) use a synthetic difference in differences over all Finnish wage earners, Hernæs and Kostøl (2026) track monthly Norwegian employment by occupation, age and exposure, and Facius and Iacono (2026) compare young workers in more and less exposed occupations within the same Norwegian firms, finding small and insignificant declines, unlike the Swedish design, whose “last in, first out” rule may explain part of the gap, and larger estimates at placebo dates before ChatGPT.

Exposure designs: wages

In an event study over 2018 to 2026 with 2022 as the base year, Azar et al. (2026a) find that moving an occupation from the 10th to the 90th percentile of exposure lowered its wages by 4.9% by 2026, with no differential trend before 2022. The employer survey gives the same estimate with no detectable change in employment (Azar et al. 2026b); exposed occupations have so far adjusted through pay rather than headcount. The composition adjusted college premium fell by 5.3 log points from 2022 to 2026. Graduates worked in more exposed occupations than workers without a degree in 2022; multiplying this gap by the estimated wage effect predicts a fall in the premium of 1.5 to 1.7 log points, or 28 to 32% of the observed fall (Azar et al. 2026a). Pay can fall in an occupation that AI makes more productive for two reasons: demand for its workers falls, or AI lets more workers meet its entry requirements and supply rises (the eligibility term of section “Main patterns”). A rise in supply would raise entry, yet entry into exposed occupations fell (section “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”), so weaker demand fits the fall in pay better.

Firm adoption studies

Adoption studies compare adopters with similar firms before and after adoption; their main threat is selection. Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) use Revelio résumé data on 65 million workers at 281,000 US firms, and classify a firm as an adopter once it posts at least one vacancy for a “GenAI integrator”, a worker hired to build GenAI into the firm’s workflows: following Hampole et al. (2025), they select postings that contain keywords such as “ChatGPT”, “large language model” or “prompt engineering”, then use a language model to keep only genuine integrator roles. By March 2025, 10,433 firms had posted one. Comparing their junior and senior employment with that of other firms, which followed parallel trends from 2015 to 2022, they find junior employment about 9% lower six quarters after adoption, because adopters hire 4 fewer juniors per quarter against an average of 5.1 before, while senior employment is unaffected. In their postings, exposed tasks also drop out of junior positions at adopters after 2023, but not out of senior ones, and junior employment falls as much in jobs that cannot be done from home. Junior tasks are the most codifiable: users hand GenAI the whole task in 52% of interactions for junior occupations against 40% for senior ones.

Other adoption designs address selection differently. Chandar and Klein Teeselink (2026), who also date adoption to the first integrator vacancy, match affiliates of adopting multinationals to similar affiliates on their characteristics before 2022 and instrument adoption with the adoption rate of other multinationals headquartered in the parent’s city: senior employment rose by 6.7%, and junior employment fell by an insignificant 2.5%. Bonin et al. (2026) date adoption to the first year a firm reports using AI, mostly for text and code generation, in Denmark’s IT use survey [note 2], and compare adopters with firms not using AI in the same industry and month, each against its own trend before 2023: employment was 11% below trend by late 2025, entirely in firms with fewer than 100 employees, and 20% below for workers under 30, whereas the Danish workplace comparison of section “Worker surveys linked to registers” finds no change. Kharazian et al. (2026) date adoption to the first of three consecutive months in which a firm spends at least $100 on AI vendors, and compare 21,559 US adopters with not yet adopting firms in the same AI spending tercile: the top third of spenders raised headcount by 10% and entry level headcount by 12%. Before GenAI, growth at adopters offset most of the fall in demand for exposed work, a scale effect (Hampole et al. 2025; Aghion et al. 2025). Demand for expert work may also grow outside the firms that sell it: from 2022 to 2025, US employment of lawyers, accountants, software developers and management analysts grew by 2.5% a year, and 99% of the 337,000 jobs added were outside their specialist industries, which Garicano (2026) reads as AI letting client firms do expert work in house; one legal AI vendor reports that its clients’ in house teams are hiring more lawyers (Junestrand, 2026) [note 3]. Both are descriptive.

Online labour markets

Comparing freelancers in writing with those in less affected occupations before and after ChatGPT, Hui et al. (2024) find monthly jobs fell by 2% and earnings by 5.2%. An AI cover letter tool cut the link between how well a letter matched the job and employers’ callbacks by 51%, so employers relied more on work histories, which entrants lack (Cui et al. 2025).

Why these patterns: theory

Exposure overstates job displacement because jobs are more than lists of tasks. AI performs steps most often in chains, so jobs with scattered exposed steps are harder to automate (Demirer et al. 2026); where tasks are bundled by judgment and relationships, AI helps without removing the worker (Garicano et al. 2026); when a job’s output is the product of the qualities of its tasks, so that one poorly done task spoils the whole, automating some tasks lets the worker reallocate time to the rest and raise their quality, and labour income can increase under partial automation (Gans and Goldfarb 2026).

Juniors are most exposed because their tasks are the most codifiable (section “Firm adoption studies”). That AI raises novices’ output most (section “Field experiments”) does not mean firms hire more of them: experiments measure output per worker, whereas hiring depends on whether cheaper output raises demand by more than AI substitutes for the worker, and in codifiable tasks substitution is the likelier winner. Juniors have traditionally paid for their training by doing routine work; if AI does that work, firms keep training them only if a fully trained expert remains far more productive than a novice, and otherwise training is cut short (Garicano and Rayo 2025).

Organisation: who decides how AI is used

Whether AI replaces workers or gives them new tasks depends on choices firms make. Acemoglu et al. (2026) classify technologies by what they do to the value of workers’ expertise. Automation hands existing tasks to machines and makes the expertise behind them obsolete; augmenting technologies make workers or machines faster at the tasks they already do; expertise levelling technologies let less expert workers perform tasks that used to require other experts, as a pulse oximeter lets a technician read blood oxygen without a blood test; and new task creating technologies generate work that needs new human expertise. Only the last is unambiguously pro worker, meaning it raises the value of workers’ skills: automation lowers labour’s share of income, and the effect of the other two on wages depends on how far prices fall as output rises. They argue that private incentives may tilt AI towards automation: managers may prefer automation that makes workers replaceable when workers earn more than their outside options or unions are strong, even if it lowers productivity, and developers build what such buyers demand. Three organisational choices decide the direction.

How do management practices shape adoption?

Management, more than access to the tool, sets how far and how deeply firms adopt AI. Among 1,292 UK firms in the Office for National Statistics’ Management and Expectations Survey, those in the top tenth of structured management scores in 2023 adopt AI at rates about 10 percentage points higher than those in the bottom tenth, while the same practices do not predict the adoption of robots, specialised equipment or specialised software, and among firms with several sites, those that let sites decide on product development are more likely to adopt AI, even at the same management score (Coyle et al. 2026). France illustrates the gap: 18% of French firms with ten or more employees used AI in 2025, against 26% in Germany; French users apply generative AI far less than American ones to internal processes (56% against 84%) and customer relations (21% against 47%), functions that require sharing data across teams and reorganising work; and 69% of French workers receive neither encouragement, tools nor training from their employer, against 44% in the US (Bergeaud and Bunel 2026). Bergeaud and Bunel (2026) attribute the gap to management practices and to the cost of reorganising. Investments in data, software and training pay off only if the firm reorganises, and reorganising is expensive in France: based on large firms’ restructuring plans, laying off a worker costs the equivalent of 38 months of salary in France, against 31 in Germany, 7 in the US and 3.3 in Denmark (Coatanlem and Coste 2025). Bergeaud and Bunel (2026) argue that these costs push French firms towards limited and reversible uses of AI. Employers must also consult the works council before deploying AI tools, which led two French courts to suspend deployments in 2025, and Bergeaud and Bunel cite evidence that stricter employment protection goes with slower adoption of technologies that require reorganisation, especially in large established firms. The flexibility of labour law therefore matters for AI through restructuring: it sets the cost of changing management practices, which is where French adoption stalls. A flexicurity system on the Danish model, which combines low job protection with generous unemployment benefits and retraining, would predict faster and deeper adoption of AI at work, although these comparisons are descriptive: countries with more flexible labour laws may also differ in management quality, firm size and sector.

Does reorganisation create new tasks?

Without reorganisation, AI saves individual time but leaves the mix of tasks unchanged. Dillon et al. (2025) randomly gave 7,137 knowledge workers in 66 firms access to Microsoft’s Copilot inside their email, meeting and writing software: over six months, workers offered the tool cut their time on email by 1.4 hours a week, 12% of their time before the experiment, but meeting time, which requires colleagues to agree on new routines, did not change significantly, and the composition of tasks did not shift. New tasks appear mainly where employers act, as in the Danish survey of section “Worker surveys linked to registers”. In the terms above, the evidence so far shows mostly automation and expertise levelling within existing jobs, and the pro worker channel runs through employers’ decisions.

Who holds knowledge?

AI moves knowledge down the hierarchy, so workers rely less on superiors and colleagues. In the model of Garicano (2000), firms are knowledge hierarchies: workers handle routine problems and pass the exceptions up to managers who know more, so cheaper access to knowledge lets workers solve more problems themselves and reduces the need for managers. Ide and Talamàs (2025) add AI to this model: AI that only assists workers benefits the least knowledgeable, while AI that works autonomously, taking over routine work or solving exceptions, benefits the most knowledgeable but raises output more. The tools studied so far are assistants, and the evidence fits the first case, from customer support to product development and software. Dell’Acqua et al. (2025) showed that among 776 Procter & Gamble professionals randomly assigned to work alone or in pairs, with or without AI, on real product development problems, an individual with AI produced solutions 0.37 standard deviations better than an individual without AI, exceeding the quality of a pair without AI (0.24). Comparing open source developers just above and just below the ranking at which GitHub granted free access to its coding assistant, Hoffmann et al. (2025) find that access moved their work towards coding and away from project management. The coding share rose by 5.4 percentage points (12.4%), and the share spent coordinating others, such as reviewing their contributions, fell by 10 points (24.9%). Before GenAI, US firms that hired more AI workers from 2010 to 2018 became flatter: a share of AI workers one standard deviation higher went with a 1.6% rise in the share of junior employees and falls of 0.8% in middle management and 0.7% in senior roles (Babina et al. 2023).

For employment, the chain runs from these organisational decisions to entry: an AI that answers juniors’ questions replaces the senior time spent training them, which can raise juniors’ output in the short run but weaken firms’ reason to hire them, which disrupts traditional knowledge hierarchies.

Evaluation

Remote work may have prepared the ground for AI by turning office tasks into digital records (Deming, 2026), and it weakens junior training: when a large firm closed its offices, younger engineers lost most of the feedback they had received from sitting near teammates (Emanuel et al., 2026). Whether remote work rather than AI explains the fall in junior hiring is disputed: remote work exposure absorbs most of the GenAI effect in exposure designs, yet at adopting firms junior employment falls as much in jobs that cannot be done from home (Lambert and Schindler, 2026; section “Firm adoption studies”). Exposed jobs were also deteriorating before ChatGPT: unemployment risk rose from early 2022 (Frank et al. 2026).

Main patterns

Combining the expertise model of Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026) with the demand condition of Lichtinger and Hosseini (2026), the change in employment in occupation o is, to a first order,

\Delta\ln L_o \approx \underbrace{b_o\,(\eta_o-\sigma_o)\,\Delta a}_{\text{substitution and scale}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{(\eta_o-1)\,\kappa_o}{\eta_o+\kappa_o}\,\pi_o}_{\text{productivity}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\eta_o}{\eta_o+\kappa_o}\,\Delta\ln ps_o}_{\text{eligibility}}

where Lₒ is employment in occupation o; Δa is the fall in the log price of AI services; bₒ is the occupation’s exposure, measured as the share of its costs spent on AI services; σₒ is the elasticity of substitution between its labour and AI services: how easily AI does the work instead of the worker; ηₒ is the price elasticity of demand for its output, the demand side: when AI makes the occupation’s output 1% cheaper, whether through cheaper AI or more productive workers, the economy buys ηₒ% more of it in place of other occupations’ output; πₒ is its workers’ log productivity gain, −ln(1−gₒ), where gₒ is the share of labour time AI saves on its tasks; psₒ is the share of workers whose training meets its entry requirement; σ is the elasticity of substitution between occupations; and κₒ is the elasticity of labour supply to the occupation’s wage [note 4].

Clear: consensus across papers or robust identification.

  • Productivity gain, πₒ. The value of this parameter draws consensus on narrow tasks: every field experiment in section “Field experiments” finds gains, largest for novices. It is measured task by task, for few occupations.

  • Exposure and diffusion, bₒΔa. Task exposure, a proxy for bₒ, is well measured: it is the GenAI occupational exposure defined in section “Key concepts and definitions”. Adoption into firms’ work has been slow so far: only 30 to 40% of US workers use GenAI at work (Hartley et al. 2026), and nine in ten executives report no effect on employment (Yotzov et al. 2026). Inflexible labour laws and high restructuring costs predict weak diffusion of AI within firms and slower integration into workflows (section “Organisation: who decides how AI is used”). Since the first term scales with bₒΔa, aggregate effects are small: total employment, earnings and hours are unchanged in Finland, Norway and Denmark (sections “Worker surveys linked to registers” and “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”).

  • Mobility, κₒ, and the margin of adjustment. The margin is robustly identified: employment adjusts through entry, not layoffs. Hiring and entry designs in the US, Sweden, the UK and France find fewer young hires in exposed jobs (section “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”). This reconciles falling entry with unchanged totals: hires are a small flow relative to the stock of workers, so the stock responds slowly. For illustration, if an occupation permanently loses a fifth of its entrants and workers stay in it for 20 years on average, its employment is about 3% lower after three years and close to 20% lower only after several decades. How displaced workers would move is not yet observed: 6.1 million US workers (4.2%) are in highly exposed occupations with low capacity to adapt (Manning and Aguirre 2026).

Debated.

  • Substitutability between AI and workers against demand, σₒ against ηₒ. The sign of the first term is debated: the adoption studies of section “Firm adoption studies” split between adopters that cut junior hiring and adopters that grew, and before GenAI the scale effect dominated. Part of the gap reflects who counts as an adopter, since spending selects firms that are already growing. The cost of automating tasks and the response of demand, which set this term, are not yet measured (Lichtinger and Hosseini 2026). The value of ηₒ is not yet identified: Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026) set it to 5 for every occupation, at the top of the range of estimates, so that a 1% fall in the price of an occupation’s output raises demand for it by 5%. At that value, cheaper AI raises an occupation’s employment unless AI replaces its workers very easily, σₒ > 5. This parameter decides whether AI is pro worker in the sense of section “Organisation: who decides how AI is used”: augmenting and expertise levelling technologies raise employment and wages only if demand expands faster than prices fall, ηₒ > 1; near or below 1, productivity gains would cut employment in the occupations AI helps most, and the wage effects below would shrink or reverse.

  • How much of Δa is GenAI. Remote work and a decline that began in early 2022 may explain part of the fall in entry (section “Evaluation”), and uncertainty may pause hiring: workers put a 20% probability on losing their job to GenAI within two years, while postings show no response (Hartley et al. 2026). Whether exposed firms that do not use AI also cut hiring, as uncertainty would predict, is not yet tested.

  • Eligibility, Δln psₒ. This value is not yet identified: the eligibility shift is measured only by language model ratings (Hosseini and Lichtinger 2026). With ηₒ = 5, productivity gains alone widen the gap between the 90th and 50th wage percentiles from 0.73 to 0.86 log points, the eligibility shift alone narrows it to 0.67, and both together widen it to 0.79. In their dynamic model, 80% of the eligibility effect takes nine years to appear, and it predicts more entry into exposed occupations, whereas entry fell.

  • Direction of change: exposure and substitutability as choices. The equation takes bₒ and σₒ as given, but firms choose whether AI automates tasks or creates new ones, and managers’ and developers’ incentives may tilt that choice towards automation. The value of the new task margin is not yet identified: so far AI saves time within existing jobs, and adoption follows management practices (section “Organisation: who decides how AI is used”).

  • Where the fall in entry ends. The long run effect on the stock is not yet identified, and it moves with the technology. The length of software tasks that frontier models complete with a 50% success rate has doubled about every seven months since 2019 (Kwa et al. 2025). If capabilities keep improving at that pace, the set of exposed tasks grows before the stock has adjusted, and the cut in entry may deepen and spread to older workers. Fewer juniors trained today also means fewer experts later (section “Why these patterns: theory”).

  • Who captures the gains, outside the equation. The buildout pays off only if AI firms capture a large share of the gains: recovering the 2025 to 2032 investment at a 10% return requires about $3.7 trillion of annual revenue by 2032, or 9.2% of projected US GDP, against about $100 billion today for OpenAI and Anthropic combined (Van Nieuwerburgh 2026). Firms’ AI bills are rising, but through volume rather than price, and a growing share of that volume goes to cheap open weight models, which anyone can download and run: on OpenRouter, a platform through which developers and firms call several hundred models, open weight models rose from 40% to 73% of the tokens processed between January and August 2026, but only from 11% to 34% of spending, and a call to an open model by French users cost 57% less than one to a closed model (Bergeaud, Delbouve, et al. 2026). So far users capture most of the value: the average US user would need $124.50 a month to give up chatbots (median $11.40), a consumer surplus of $172 billion a year against about $14 billion of global consumer revenue (Brynjolfsson et al. 2026). Revenue on that scale would therefore have to come from firms paying for AI in place of labour. Acemoglu (2026) sees a dilemma: if that revenue arrives, income shifts from workers to the owners of AI, as with past automation, which accounts for 50 to 70% of the change in the US wage structure over four decades (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2022); if it does not, the boom risks a costly crash [note 5].
