The four-day school week: what does the evidence say?

About this page. This page was produced in one session using the policy-analysis method on this site: a research agent searched, read 12 studies and official documents in full, and recorded 73 figures directly from the sources into an evidence ledger. This summary uses 62 of those figures, each linked to its ledger ID, while dropping press-only figures from two US teacher-retention studies. It illustrates the method rather than providing a full review of the literature. Small grey ids after each figure link to its row in the evidence ledger (CSV).

The question

Some schools teach four longer days instead of five, including about 1,607 US schools across 24 states in 2019 L37, and most French primary schools since 2008, apart from the 2013 to 2017 period. Does a shorter week change what pupils learn, what schools spend, who teaches, and how parents work?

What changes

  • Days and hours. In a RAND sample of US districts, four-day districts taught 146 days a year compared with 171 L23. Their school day was 49 minutes longer L24, which still resulted in 58 fewer hours a year (1,156 against 1,214) L22. In Oregon, weekly time in school was nearly 2.5 hours shorter L08.
  • France. The 2008 four-day week set 24 hours of teaching a week over 144 days L49, down from 26 hours a week L50. The 2013 reform shortened each day by 45 minutes on average and added a Wednesday half day L47, keeping annual hours unchanged at 864 L51. Once a 2017 decree allowed schools to opt out, about 84% of public-school timetables (covering 82% of pupils) had returned to four days by July 2018 L53.
  • Cost lines cited for the switch. Among 342 US districts, 65.1% gave financial savings as a main reason for adopting the schedule L16. Districts expected to save on transport, heating and support-staff pay, though not on teacher contracts (Morton 2021, p. 30).

Three of the five US achievement studies find learning losses, mostly of a few hundredths of a standard deviation L06, L07, L33, L25, with larger losses in non-rural schools L36 and after many years L27, L28. One study finds no effect L13. The single positive finding comes from Colorado, where four-day schools are mostly small and rural and cover about 3% of pupils L01, L05. Financial savings are real but small, typically 0 to 3% of the budget L30. The French evidence examines the reverse move from four days to 4.5 days and its effect on parents' work, rather than pupils' learning.

What we don't know

  • France, learning outcomes. There is no causal estimate of how the 2008 move to four days, or the 2013 and 2017 reversals, affected pupils' learning. The ministry's 2017 evaluation compared timetables, though it could not identify the reform's causal effect L52.
  • Long-run and non-rural effects. Most US adopters are small and rural. The largest losses appear in non-rural schools and after many years L27, L36, which is where samples are thinnest.
  • Teacher supply. Only one causal study of teacher turnover was read in full L38, and RAND did not measure recruitment L31. Studies from other states were available only through press reports and are excluded here.
  • Families. The US evidence on parents' employment comes from a single dissertation abstract L55. Causal effects on childcare costs and on how children spend the day off have not been measured.
  • Version risk. Several figures come from working-paper versions L06, L33, L38, L42, and published versions may report different estimates.
